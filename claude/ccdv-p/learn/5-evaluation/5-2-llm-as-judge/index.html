    Engineering LLM Judges

    Exam skillLLM-as-Judge Engineering

    Analyzing LLM-as-judge evaluator quality against human-labeled data, including calibration, rubric design, bias and position effects, inter-rater reliability, and judge drift across model versions.

    Treat an LLM judge as a measurement instrument, with a rubric clear enough for humans to agree on, agreement with human labels measured by category and corrected for chance, known biases controlled, and recalibration whenever the judge's model changes.

    Written and maintained by Prakash Sridharan, Claude Certified Architect

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    An LLM judge scales evaluation to thousands of outputs, but its verdicts mean something only if they agree with careful human judgment. A judge that has never been checked against human labels is an opinion, and gating releases on it can make quality worse while every release passes.

    Calibrate before you gate

    1. Write a rubric with specific, observable criteria and anchored examples of each score.
    2. Check that humans agree with each other using the rubric. If two raters disagree on 40% of items, fix the rubric first; a judge cannot be calibrated to labels that disagree.
    3. Measure judge agreement with human labels using chance-corrected statistics (such as Cohen's kappa), by category as well as overall.
    4. Give gating authority only where agreement is acceptable. Elsewhere, keep humans in the loop or improve the rubric.
    Example
    92% overall, 55% where it mattered

    A judge agreed with humans 92% of the time overall, but 55% on refund-policy exceptions. It could gate routine cases, not exceptions, until its rubric for exceptions improved.

    Known biases and their controls

    BiasSignControl
    PositionIn pairwise comparison, picks the first (or second) response more often than humans doRandomize order, or run both orders and count a win only when both agree
    VerbosityScores longer answers higher at equal qualityRubric criteria that give no credit for length; re-measure on labeled pairs
    Self-preferenceRates outputs from its own model family higher than humans doMeasure agreement per source model; use a judge from a different family for those comparisons
    Missing referenceGrades factual accuracy without the source documentGive the judge the reference material

    Truncating answers to equal length removes content the judge needs; using a larger judge does not guarantee the biases disappear.

    Output formats that calibrate well

    Broad numeric scales (1 to 10) tend to cluster and drift, and a 0.2-point change is hard to interpret. Prefer pass or fail checks on defined criteria, each with a short reason. Reasons make verdicts auditable: spot-check them against human review to find judge errors.

    For pairwise verdicts run in both orders, inconsistent pairs are ties, and the rate of inconsistency is itself a calibration signal for position bias.

    Drift and gaming

    • Judge drift across model versions: moving the judge to a new snapshot changes what its scores mean. Re-run calibration on the human-labeled set and reset thresholds before it gates again.
    • Optimizing against the judge: if the pass rate rises steadily while human spot checks stay flat, prompts may have been tuned toward what the judge rewards. Periodic recalibration on fresh human labels detects both this and drift.

    Judges for safety-critical screening

    When a judge screens for a dangerous failure (missing medication information in discharge summaries), calibrate for recall on that failure against expert labels, set the threshold to favor recall, and keep sampling the cases it passes for human review. Average agreement can hide misses on rare, dangerous cases.

    Exam trap
    Lowering the threshold

    When an uncalibrated judge passes everything, lowering the pass threshold makes it more permissive, not more sensitive. Calibration comes before any threshold change.

    In the exam

    Judge items show agreement data, bias evidence or a gate that passes everything. Pick the step that establishes or restores calibration first: control the bias, re-measure against humans, fix the rubric, recalibrate after a model change, withhold gating authority. Distractors switch to a bigger judge, change the scale, lower thresholds, average inconsistent verdicts, or replace human labels with the judge.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    A pairwise judge agrees with humans just over half the time and favors the first response. What should the team do first?

    Show answer

    Answer: C. Control the bias and re-establish calibration first; other changes are untested until then.

    Question 2

    Two human raters using the rubric disagree on 40% of replies. What comes first?

    Show answer

    Answer: A. A judge cannot be calibrated to labels that disagree; the rubric must produce human agreement first.

    Question 3

    A judge used for gating moves to a new snapshot. What should happen?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Judges drift across versions; recalibration restores what the thresholds mean.

    Apply it: Calibrate Cobalt's summary judge

    Exercise · about 25 minCase: Cobalt Health Network

    Cobalt wants an LLM judge to score visit summaries before release changes, and to screen summaries for missing medication changes. Clinicians return 7% of summaries for correction today. The team has 600 summaries labeled by two clinicians, who agreed on 81% of them.

    1. Write three rubric criteria that are specific and observable.
    2. Design the calibration study and what it reports.
    3. Choose the bias controls the judge needs.
    4. Set the rules for the medication screen.
      Done when: a missed medication change is unlikely to pass unseen.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Criteria (pass or fail each, with a one-line reason).

    • Every medication started, stopped or changed in the transcript appears in the summary with dose and reason.
    • Every follow-up instruction given to the patient appears in the patient letter.
    • No statement in the summary is unsupported by the transcript.

    2. Calibration. Resolve the 19% clinician disagreements by adjudication to make a reference set. Run the judge on all 600 with the transcript provided, and report chance-corrected agreement per criterion and per visit type (acute, chronic, behavioral health). The judge gates only criteria and visit types where agreement meets the agreed level.

    3. Bias controls. The judge always receives the transcript (no missing reference). The rubric gives no credit for length. Because Claude writes the summaries, agreement is reported separately for Claude-written and clinician-written summaries, and a judge from a different model family is evaluated if self-preference shows.

    4. Medication screen. Calibrated for recall on medication changes against clinician labels; the threshold is set so recall is at least 98% on the reference set, accepting more flags. A weekly sample of 50 passed summaries goes to a clinician, and any miss becomes a regression case.
