    Choosing a Model Tier and Planning Upgrades

    Exam skillModel Tier Selection and Release Planning

    Evaluating model tier options across quality, latency, and cost against task requirements and binding constraints, justifying the selection, and planning for behavior change across model releases using regression evaluations.

    Choose the model tier that meets a task's quality bar under its binding constraint (latency, cost, platform, error tolerance), justify it with an evaluation, route mixed traffic to different tiers, and plan model upgrades with pinned versions, regression evaluations, gradual rollout and a rollback path.

    Written and maintained by Prakash Sridharan, Claude Certified Architect

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    Three trade-offs, one binding constraint

    Larger tiers are more capable, slower and costlier; smaller tiers are faster and cheaper. Find the constraint that cannot move, then pick the cheapest tier that passes the quality bar under it.

    SituationBinding constraintLikely choice
    Autocomplete, 400 ms, millions of requestsLatency and volumeSmallest tier that passes
    Contract risk analysis, 200 a day, costly errorsQualityCapable tier, confirmed on real contracts
    2 million records to label by next weekCost and volumeSmall tier through batch, after a quality check
    Must run on Vertex AI for compliancePlatformWhat that platform offers, designed around its features
    Internal, low-risk summaries; 88% vs 91% at five times the priceCostSmall tier
    Decision rule
    Justify with data

    A tier choice is defensible when it is backed by an evaluation set of real inputs. "Newest" and "biggest is safest" are not justifications.

    Route mixed traffic

    When 80% of requests are simple FAQs and 20% are complex investigations, route each kind to its own tier. Smaller models often match larger ones on focused tasks when given examples and, for hard cases, thinking.

    Total cost, not token price

    A cheaper tier that causes retries, escalations or human rework can cost more overall. Measure cost per successful outcome.

    Planning for releases

    StepWhy
    Pin a dated model ID in productionBehavior changes only when you decide
    Track deprecation datesRetired models stop working
    Run the regression set on the new versionFind changes that matter to your prompts
    Compare quality, cost, latency, tool use and formatUpgrades can change verbosity and tool-call habits
    Adjust prompts, schema or effortSmall changes usually close the gaps
    Roll out gradually with monitoringProblems show on a small share first
    Keep a rollback to the previous pinned version while it existsFast recovery
    Exam trap
    "It passed overall"

    A new version that passes overall but changes formatting in 4% of cases will break a parser. The 4% is the finding; fix it before rollout.

    In the exam

    Items give task requirements, constraints and evaluation numbers and ask for the tier or the release plan. Identify the binding constraint, prefer the cheapest tier that meets the bar, route mixed traffic, and plan upgrades with pinning, regression testing and gradual rollout. Distractors pick by newness or size, switch everything at once, or stay on a model past retirement.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    A 400 ms autocomplete serves millions of short requests. Which tier?

    Show answer

    Answer: C. Latency and volume dominate; quality only needs to meet the bar.

    Question 2

    A pinned model retires in six months. What now?

    Show answer

    Answer: A. Pinning buys stability, not permanence.

    Question 3

    After an upgrade, quality holds but cost rises 30% from more tool calls. Release-plan step?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Upgrades change behavior beyond quality; measure and adjust before full rollout.

    Apply it: Choose tiers for Juniper

    Exercise · about 20 minCase: Juniper Outdoor Co.

    Juniper's agent runs on Tier S, and disputes are handled poorly. A consultant proposes moving all traffic to Tier L. Traffic: 75% order-status and return questions, 20% product advice, 5% complex disputes. The review classifier runs on Tier M in realtime. A new model version is announced; the current one retires in five months.

    1. Propose a tier for each kind of traffic and how you would justify it.
    2. Compare the monthly cost of the consultant's all-Tier-L plan with your routing, using the case's assumed prices for 70,000 conversations a month averaging 60,000 input and 1,500 output tokens (before caching).
    3. Write the upgrade plan.
      Done when: every choice is tied to a constraint and an evaluation.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Order status and returns: stay on Tier S. Product advice: Tier M. Disputes: Tier L. Review classifier: Tier S through batch. Each is confirmed on a set of 100 to 200 real cases per traffic type, comparing tiers on the same cases.

    2. All Tier L: input 70,000 x 60,000 = 4.2B tokens x $15 per million = $63,000; output 105M x $75 = $7,875; about $70,900 a month. Routed: Tier S (75%) 3.15B x $1 + 78.75M x $5 = about $3,540; Tier M (20%) 840M x $3 + 21M x $15 = about $2,840; Tier L (5%) 210M x $15 + 5.25M x $75 = about $3,540; about $9,900 a month. Today, all Tier S, is about $4,700: routing roughly doubles today's cost to fix disputes and advice, where the all-L plan multiplies it by fifteen. Caching (lesson 5.3) cuts all three figures further.

    3. Pin the current versions; run each traffic type's evaluation on the new version; compare quality, cost, tool calls and format; adjust prompts; roll out at 10%, 50%, 100% over two weeks with dashboards; keep the old pins as rollback until migration is complete, at least a month before retirement.
