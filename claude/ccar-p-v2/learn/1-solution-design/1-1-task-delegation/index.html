    Delegating Work Between Claude and People

    Exam skillTask Delegation Between Claude and Humans

    Determining which components of a described business process should be delegated to Claude, retained by humans, or handled collaboratively, and justifying each assignment against reversibility, stakes, and accountability.

    Decide which parts of a business process Claude does, which stay with existing systems, and which stay with people, and justify each choice by reversibility, stakes and accountability.

    Written and maintained by Prakash Sridharan, Claude Certified Architect

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    Delegation is the step between "here is the business process" and "here is the architecture". The exam gives you a process and asks who should own each step. Done well, every step goes to the owner that does it reliably, people stay accountable for the decisions that need them, and nothing is left to the model just because it could attempt it.

    Three owners

    OwnerWhat it should ownExamples
    ClaudeLanguage work: reading, extracting, classifying, summarizing, planning, draftingRead a free-text complaint and identify the product and issue
    Existing systemsDeterministic logic and authoritative data the business already trustsAccount balances, eligibility rules, pricing, the record of truth
    PeopleJudgment, exceptions, approvals, decisions someone must answer forApproving a large refund, deciding an unusual claim

    A fourth arrangement, collaborative, is common: Claude prepares and a person decides. Claude drafts the credit decision, the summary or the plan; the person reviews and acts. This keeps people accountable without making them do the reading and drafting.

    The most frequent mistake is to give everything to Claude because it can attempt everything. Over-assigning makes the system more expensive, slower, harder to audit and less accurate on precisely the tasks existing systems already do well. The opposite mistake, keeping everything with people "to be safe", wastes them on low-risk work and stalls the process.

    Decide by reversibility, stakes and accountability

    For each step, ask three questions.

    1. Can it be undone? A draft, a summary, a classification or a reversible record update can be corrected later. Money sent, a charge posted, a message delivered or a record deleted often cannot.
    2. What does a mistake cost? A wrong tag on a ticket is cheap; a wrong payout, a wrong dose or a wrong legal statement is not.
    3. Who must answer for it? Some decisions must be attributable to a named, authorized person: releasing funds, a hiring or lending decision, a legal hold, a clinical sign-off.
    AnswersDelegation
    Reversible, low stakes, no named owner requiredClaude acts; sample after the fact
    Reversible but costly to get wrongClaude prepares; a person or a check in code confirms
    Irreversible or high stakesA person approves before it happens
    Accountability must sit with a personThe person decides; Claude assists
    Example
    Outage credits after a storm

    A utility's agent proposes bill credits for thousands of outage claims. Credits under a set limit post automatically, and a sample is audited daily; larger credits, which are hard to claw back once a bill is issued, wait for a person. Auditing every credit after the billing cycle would find mistakes too late, and reviewing every small credit would leave thousands of customers waiting.

    Values belong to systems and code

    Decision rule
    Numbers belong to code

    Totals, interest, tax, dosage, dates between events: anything that must be exactly right is computed deterministically, by an existing system or by code execution. Claude can explain the result; it should not be the calculator.

    For every piece Claude owns, check it against the model's limits. Precise values come from a system or from code. Current or specialized knowledge comes in through retrieval or a tool. Input that does not fit the context is split or loaded progressively. An instruction that is vague is sharpened, or the logic moves into code.

    Example
    Warranty claim handling
    • Read the customer's description and photos and identify the fault: Claude.
    • Check whether the product is still under warranty: the warranty system.
    • Calculate the refund or replacement value: the pricing service, not the model.
    • Draft the customer response: Claude.
    • Approve claims above a value threshold or outside policy: a person.

    Put people where the stakes are

    Human steps are part of the design, not a patch added at the end. Place approval at the point where a decision becomes consequential or irreversible, such as issuing money, changing a record of truth, or sending something on the company's behalf. Routine, low-risk outputs flow without review, which keeps reviewers focused on what matters. Lesson 5.4 covers how to build those gates so they do not stall the rest of the work.

    Exam trap
    "Let Claude check the rules"

    When a scenario includes a published policy, a rules table or an eligibility service, options that ask Claude to apply those rules from memory or from the prompt are usually distractors. The existing system applies the rule; Claude handles the language around it.

    Exam trap
    Confidence as the gate

    Options that let the model decide when it needs a person, by reporting its own confidence, are distractors for high-stakes steps. A self-reported score is not a control.

    In the exam

    Delegation items describe a business process, often as a list of steps, and ask which assignment is most defensible, or which TWO steps must stay with a person. The right answer gives language work to Claude, authoritative data and rules to existing systems, and irreversible, high-stakes or accountable decisions to people, with low-risk steps left to run. Watch for options that rely on an audit after the fact for something that cannot be undone, send every step to a person, or let the model's confidence decide who approves.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    In a claims process, which task should stay with the existing policy administration system?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Whether a policy was in force is an authoritative fact held by the system of record. The other three are language tasks that suit Claude.

    Question 2

    A lending assistant must quote the monthly repayment for a given rate and term. How should the figure be produced?

    Show answer

    Answer: C. Exact figures belong to deterministic code or the system that owns the calculation. Claude adds value by explaining the result, not by being the calculator.

    Question 3 · select 2

    An HR agent offboards employees. Which TWO steps must stay with an accountable person?

    Show answer

    Answer: B, D. A disputed payment and a legal hold are decisions an organization must attribute to an accountable person. Summaries and checklists are language work Claude can prepare for review.

    Apply it: Decide who owns each step

    Exercise · about 15 minCase: Lakeview Regional Medical Center

    Lakeview wants Claude to help with discharge summaries. Take the seven steps of today's process from the case and decide who should own each one: Claude, an existing system, or a person.

    1. Assign an owner to each of the seven steps, with a one-line reason.
      Why: over-assigning to the model is the most common delegation mistake.
    2. List the values that must come from a system of record, never from the model.
      Done when: every value a wrong guess could harm a patient with is on the list.
    3. Place the human checkpoint and justify it by reversibility, stakes and accountability.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Owners

    StepOwnerReason
    Gather the stay's notesEHR (FHIR API)Retrieval of records is a system task
    Write the hospital course, list diagnoses and proceduresClaude draftsSummarizing long notes is a language task
    Discharge medication listPharmacy systemThe reconciled list is authoritative; Claude copies it in, never edits it
    Pending resultsEHR queryA live fact, not something to infer
    Follow-up instructions and appointmentsClaude drafts the instructions; scheduling system booksPlain-language writing suits Claude; booking is a transaction
    Review and signPhysicianClinical accountability, required by policy
    Assign codesCertified coders, using the coding systemCoding is a regulated professional task

    2. Values from systems of record: patient identity, medications and doses, allergies, pending results, appointment dates and times, and the signing physician.

    3. Checkpoint. The physician reviews before anything is sent: once a summary reaches the patient and the next provider it cannot be recalled, an error can harm the patient, and the summary must carry a named clinician's signature. The review screen shows the draft next to the source notes, with each statement in the hospital course linked to the note it came from, the medication list marked "from pharmacy, not editable here", and a clear "Sign" action that records who signed and when.
