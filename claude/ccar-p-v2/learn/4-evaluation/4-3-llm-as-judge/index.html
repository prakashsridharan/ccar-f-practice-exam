    LLM-as-Judge Design and Bias

    Exam skillLLM-as-Judge Design and Bias

    Evaluating the design of an LLM-as-judge or rubric-based evaluation whose results are inconsistent with human review, and selecting the correction that addresses the responsible bias (position, verbosity, self-preference, judge drift).

    Design model-graded evaluations that agree with human review, and when they do not, find the bias responsible (position, verbosity, self-preference or judge drift) and apply the correction that addresses it.

    Written and maintained by Prakash Sridharan, Claude Certified Architect

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    An LLM judge (a model that grades another model's output) makes it possible to evaluate thousands of open-ended answers that no exact-match test can score. It is also a model, with predictable biases. The exam gives you a judge whose results disagree with human review and asks which bias is responsible and which correction fixes it.

    Design a judge that can be trusted

    • Grade against a rubric, not a feeling. Specific criteria ("states the refund window from the policy", "does not promise a delivery date") with defined scores give consistent verdicts; "rate the quality from 1 to 10" does not.
    • Ask for the reasoning before the score, so the verdict follows from the criteria.
    • Use code where code can grade. Exact fields, formats, required citations and banned phrases are checked deterministically; the judge handles what needs judgement.
    • Calibrate against people. Keep a set of cases labelled by human reviewers, measure how often the judge agrees, and fix the judge before trusting it on new results.

    Four biases and their corrections

    BiasWhat it looks likeCorrection
    PositionIn pairwise comparisons the judge favours whichever answer it sees first (or second)Judge each pair twice with the order swapped; count only verdicts that agree
    VerbosityThe judge prefers longer answers, even when people prefer the short, correct oneA rubric of specific criteria that gives no credit for length on its own; check scores against answer length
    Self-preferenceThe judge scores outputs from its own model higher than people doUse a judge from a different model than the one being judged, and check against human labels
    Judge driftScores change with no change in the outputs, after the judge model or its prompt changed, or agreement with people falls over timePin the judge's model version; recalibrate against a fixed human-labelled set before trusting new scores
    Decision rule
    A judge is a measurement instrument

    Calibrate it against people before you trust it, pin it so it stays the same instrument, and re-check it whenever it changes. A drifting judge makes a stable system look like it is improving or regressing.

    Read the symptom carefully

    The audit data in an item usually points to one bias.

    • "Picks the first answer in 68% of pairs" or "the verdict flips when the order is reversed": position.
    • "Prefers the longer answer even when reviewers rate the shorter one complete": verbosity.
    • "Rates its own model's answers higher than reviewers do": self-preference.
    • "Scores for unchanged outputs rose after the judge was updated" or "agreement with the monthly human sample fell from 90% to 78%": drift.
    Example
    A judge that rewarded padding

    A documentation team used a model judge to compare two prompt versions. The version that won produced answers 40% longer, but human reviewers preferred the other version for accuracy and focus. The judge's rubric said "rate helpfulness", which rewarded detail. Rewriting the rubric as five specific criteria with no credit for length, and swapping answer order on every pair, brought the judge's agreement with reviewers from 70% to 91%.

    Exam trap
    More randomness as a fix

    Raising the judge's temperature makes its verdicts noisier; it does not remove a systematic preference.

    Exam trap
    Dropping the human comparison

    When the judge and reviewers disagree, removing the reviewers removes the only evidence of the problem.

    In the exam

    Judge items describe an LLM-as-judge or rubric-based evaluation whose results are inconsistent with human review, often with audit figures, and ask for the correction. Name the bias from the evidence (order effects, length preference, favouring its own model, change over time), then pick its correction: swapped order, criteria that ignore length, a different judge model, or recalibration against a fixed human-labelled set with a pinned judge. Distractors use the generating model as its own judge, raise temperature, or stop comparing with people.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    In pairwise comparisons, a judge picks the answer shown first in 65% of pairs, while reviewers split evenly. Which correction addresses this?

    Show answer

    Answer: B. A preference for the first position is position bias. Swapping the order and keeping only consistent verdicts cancels it.

    Question 2

    After the judge's model alias moved to a newer model, scores for outputs that had not changed rose by 6 points. What should the team do?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Scores that move without any change in the outputs show judge drift. Pinning keeps the instrument stable, and recalibration shows whether the new judge can be trusted.

    Question 3 · select 2

    Which TWO practices make a model judge more reliable?

    Show answer

    Answer: A, C. Specific criteria give consistent verdicts, and agreement with human labels shows whether the judge can be trusted. Unexplained scores and self-grading hide bias.

    Apply it: Fix Hollis & Grant's reply judge

    Exercise · about 15 minCase: Hollis & Grant Home

    Hollis & Grant uses a model judge to score the support agent's draft replies before each prompt release. This month, human reviewers rated a sample of 300 replies. The judge agreed with them on 71% of replies. Replies over 200 words scored 1.4 points higher on the judge's 10-point scale than reviewers gave them, and the judge is the same model that drafts the replies. The judge's alias was updated two weeks ago.

    1. Name the biases the evidence points to, citing the figure for each.
    2. Redesign the judge: rubric, model, order handling where relevant, and version handling.
      Done when: every named bias has a correction.
    3. Set the acceptance rule for trusting the judge in release decisions.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Biases. Verbosity: long replies score 1.4 points higher than reviewers rate them. Self-preference: the judge is the drafting model, so it may favour its own style. Possible drift: the alias moved two weeks ago, so this month's scores may not be comparable with last month's.

    2. Redesign.

    BiasCorrection
    VerbosityA rubric of five criteria (states the policy correctly, answers the question asked, gives the next step, keeps card data out, tone fits the brand), each scored pass or fail, with no credit for length
    Self-preferenceA judge from a different model than the drafting model
    DriftJudge pinned to a dated model version; any change re-runs the calibration set first

    Deterministic checks handle what code can: card numbers absent, order number format, required links present.

    3. Acceptance rule. The judge is used for release decisions only while its agreement with the 300-reply human-labelled set is at least 85%, with no criterion below 80%. It is re-checked monthly and after any change to its model or rubric.
