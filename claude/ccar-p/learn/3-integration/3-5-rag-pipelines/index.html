Designing a RAG Pipeline

Exam taskDesign a RAG pipeline with appropriate chunking and indexing strategies

Design a retrieval pipeline end to end, choosing chunking by the shape of the documents and indexing by how people ask, and keep the index fresh and measured.

What you need to know

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) answers questions from a body of knowledge too large, too specialized or too changeable to rely on the model's training. The pipeline has two halves:

  • Indexing (offline): collect documents, split them into chunks, compute representations, and store them in an index.
  • Answering (online): turn the question into a query, retrieve candidate chunks, optionally rerank them, and pass the best ones to Claude with instructions to answer from them and cite.

Remember lesson 1.2: retrieval is for stable knowledge. Live state comes from tools.

Do you need RAG at all?

If the whole knowledge base fits comfortably in the context window (a few hundred pages at most), loading it directly, with prompt caching, can be simpler and more accurate than building a pipeline. RAG earns its complexity when the corpus is large, grows, or changes.

Chunking: by the shape of the documents

The chunk is the unit that comes back. Choose the method by what the documents are, not by a default size.

MethodHowFits
Fixed-size with overlapEqual spans, overlapping a littleUniform, unstructured text with weak natural boundaries
SemanticSplit where the topic changesProse, where each chunk must make sense on its own
Structural (hierarchical)Follow the document's sections and subsectionsManuals, contracts, policies, where the section heading carries meaning

A common failure: a chunk loses the context that made it meaningful ("the limit is 30 days", but for which policy?). Fixes include keeping section titles and document names with each chunk, and contextual retrieval, which prepends a short, generated description of where the chunk sits in its document before indexing it.

Indexing: by how people ask

IndexMatchesFits
Dense (embeddings)Meaning, even with different wordsQuestions phrased unlike the source text
Sparse (keyword, such as BM25)Exact terms: part numbers, codes, namesQuestions that hinge on a specific identifier
HybridBoth, with results mergedMixed query patterns, which is most production traffic

Three trade-offs to name

Every retrieval design balances retrieval quality (does the right chunk come back?), latency (how much time retrieval adds) and maintenance (how much it costs to keep the index correct as documents change). Reranking improves quality and adds latency; finer chunking improves precision and increases index size; frequent re-indexing improves freshness and costs compute.

Decision rule
Measure retrieval separately from answers

Keep a set of questions with the passages that should be retrieved. Track whether the right chunk appears in the top results, not only whether the final answer looks right. A good answer from a lucky retrieval will not stay good.

Keeping it fresh

Decide how changes reach the index: re-index changed documents on publish, on a schedule, or both; remove withdrawn documents; record the source version in each chunk so citations point to the right edition.

Example
Policies that lost their headings

An HR assistant split a 300-page handbook into 500-token chunks. Questions about parental leave sometimes retrieved a paragraph about sick leave, because the chunk ("eligible after 26 weeks...") had lost its section heading. Re-chunking by section, keeping the heading path with each chunk, fixed most of the misses.

Exam trap
Tuning retrieval for live data

If the question is about something that changes on its own (stock, order status, balances), no chunking or index choice is the right answer. That data comes from a tool call.

Exam trap
Default chunk size for every corpus

Options that apply one fixed chunk size to structured documents (contracts, manuals) without keeping their structure are usually wrong when the scenario mentions answers that mix up sections.

In the exam

RAG design questions give a corpus and a query pattern, then ask for chunking and indexing choices, or describe a failure (wrong section retrieved, exact codes not found, stale answers) and ask for the fix. Match chunking to document shape, indexing to query pattern (hybrid when identifiers matter), and freshness to how often the documents change. If the corpus is small, consider whether RAG is needed at all.

Check your understanding

Question 1

Technicians search a manual library using exact fault codes such as "E-4417". Embedding search often returns similar-sounding but wrong codes. What fixes this?

Show answer

Answer: D. Exact identifiers are what keyword (sparse) indexes match well. A hybrid index keeps the semantic matching for other questions and recovers exact codes.

Question 2

Answers about insurance policies mix up conditions from different sections. The policies are split into fixed 400-token chunks. What is the best fix?

Show answer

Answer: C. Structured documents should be chunked along their structure, with the context that gives each chunk its meaning. Smaller fixed chunks lose even more context.

Question 3 · select 2

A team has 120 pages of reference material that rarely changes and is used on every request. Which TWO options are sound?

Show answer

Answer: A, C. A small, stable corpus may fit the context window, where caching makes it cheap. Retrieval should be added only if measurement shows a need.

Apply it: Design Corvane's product knowledge pipeline

Exercise · about 20 minCase: Corvane Home

Corvane's product-question route needs retrieval over 2,300 product manuals (PDFs with sections such as assembly, care and dimensions) and 400 help-centre articles updated weekly. Customers ask in their own words ("my table wobbles"), and sometimes quote product codes ("CV-2210-OAK").

  1. Choose chunking for each source, with a reason.
  2. Choose the index type and justify it with the query patterns.
  3. Design freshness: how revised manuals and weekly article updates reach the index, and how citations stay correct.
  4. Define two retrieval metrics you will track.
    Done when: each metric can be computed without reading every answer by hand.
Check your work
Show model answer

1. Chunking

SourceChunkingReason
Product manualsStructural: by section, with product name, product code and section path attachedAnswers depend on the section ("assembly" vs "care") and on which product
Help-centre articlesOne chunk per article, split by heading only when longArticles are short and self-contained

Contextual descriptions are generated for manual chunks so a chunk like "tighten bolts B and C" carries "Assembly, CV-2210 dining table".

2. Index. Hybrid: dense embeddings for problem descriptions in customers' words, plus keyword matching for product codes and part letters, with results merged and the top few reranked before generation.

3. Freshness. Manuals are re-indexed when the catalogue marks a product revision, removing the old version's chunks; articles are re-indexed on publish each week. Each chunk stores the document ID and version, and answers cite them, so a citation always points to the edition that was used.

4. Metrics. Recall at 5 on a labelled set of 300 real questions (is a correct chunk among the top five?), and the share of answers whose citation points to the right product, measured automatically from the product code in the question and in the cited chunk.
