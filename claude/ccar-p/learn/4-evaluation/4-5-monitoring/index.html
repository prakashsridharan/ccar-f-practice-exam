    Monitoring with Logging and Observability

    Exam taskMonitor system performance using logging and observability tools

    Keep watching quality, latency and cost after launch, using logs, sampled online evaluation, outcome signals and alerts, and feed what you find back into the evaluation set.

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    A system that passed its evaluation can still drift in production: the inputs change, documents are updated, users find new ways to ask, upstream systems change their formats. Monitoring is how you notice before customers or auditors do.

    What to monitor

    AreaSignals
    QualitySampled online evaluation scores, human-review outcomes, user corrections, escalation and override rates
    ReliabilityError rates, timeouts, tool failures, stop reasons (especially output-limit and context-exceeded), retries
    Latency50th and 95th percentile total time and time to first token, per route
    CostTokens and cost per request and per successful outcome, cache hit rate, per route and per tenant
    Safety and securityGuardrail triggers, blocked actions, injection attempts detected, policy violations found in review

    Online evaluation

    Offline test sets do not see this week's traffic. Online evaluation grades a sample of real production outputs with the same graders: code checks on everything that can be checked cheaply, a model judge on a sample, and human review on a smaller sample plus anything flagged. Trends in these scores are the earliest warning of drift.

    Classify outputs, not only errors

    A useful habit is to sort reviewed outputs into three bins:

    • Acceptable: meets the criteria.
    • Needs revision: fixable by regenerating or editing.
    • Needs override: a person or a different path must take over.

    The rates of each bin, by route and slice, show where the system is weakening. Every "needs override" case is a candidate for the golden dataset.

    Alerts that mean something

    Alert on changes from a baseline, sized to matter, sustained for long enough to rule out noise: escalation rate up 4 points for a day, cost per success up 30% for an hour, 95th-percentile latency over target for 15 minutes, any zero-tolerance violation at once. Each alert needs an owner and a first step.

    Decision rule
    Pin versions so changes are deliberate

    Use specific model versions rather than moving aliases in production, and record prompt and configuration versions with every request. Then every shift in a metric can be tied to a change you made, or ruled out as one.

    Close the loop

    Monitoring that only produces dashboards changes nothing. The loop is: detect, diagnose (lesson 4.4), fix, add the case to the evaluation set, and confirm the metric recovers.

    Example
    A drift nobody deployed

    A product assistant's sampled faithfulness score fell from 96% to 89% over three weeks with no code, prompt or model change. Change attribution showed the help-centre team had restructured 80 articles into longer pages, and the chunking no longer split them well. Re-chunking restored the score, and the content team's publishing checklist gained a step to notify the AI team of structural changes.

    Exam trap
    Monitoring only uptime and errors

    A system that answers every request quickly and wrongly is green on an error dashboard. Quality and outcome signals must be monitored too.

    Exam trap
    Floating model aliases in production

    If the model behind an alias changes, behaviour can shift without any change on your side. Production should pin versions and upgrade deliberately through evaluation.

    In the exam

    Monitoring questions describe a quality drop, a cost spike or a silent change, and ask what should have caught it or what to add. Strong answers include online evaluation on sampled traffic, outcome signals, baselines with meaningful thresholds, pinned and logged versions, and a loop back into the evaluation set.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    A summarization service has had no errors for months, yet reviewers say summaries have become less accurate. What monitoring was missing?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Quality drift does not raise errors. Grading a sample of live outputs with the same criteria as the offline evaluation shows the trend.

    Question 2

    An assistant's behaviour changed overnight with no deploy on the team's side. The application calls a model alias rather than a pinned version. What should change?

    Show answer

    Answer: C. A moving alias can change the model underneath the application. Pinning makes model changes deliberate and testable.

    Question 3 · select 2

    Which TWO alert conditions are well designed?

    Show answer

    Answer: A, C. Good alerts compare against a baseline with a meaningful, sustained threshold, or fire immediately on zero-tolerance events. Single-request and any-change alerts are noise.

    Apply it: Monitor Prairie State's intake in production

    Exercise · about 15 minCase: Prairie State Mutual

    Prairie State's intake system goes live next month. Design its monitoring.

    1. Choose two signals for each area: quality, reliability, latency, cost, safety.
    2. Design online evaluation: what is checked on every claim, what is sampled, and who reviews.
      Done when: you know how many claims a week a person looks at.
    3. Write four alerts, each with a threshold, an owner and a first step.
    4. Describe how findings reach the evaluation set.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Signals

    AreaSignals
    QualityHandler correction rate per field; sampled extraction accuracy against the source
    ReliabilityClaims stuck in the queue over 4 hours; Policy Admin call failures
    LatencyTime from receipt to acknowledgement (against the one-day SLA); extraction 95th percentile
    CostCost per claim; share of claims over 20 pages
    SafetyAny acknowledgement mentioning a decision on cover; any EU claim processed outside the EU route

    2. Online evaluation. Code checks on every claim: schema valid, key fields match Policy Admin, no decision language, correct regional route. A model judge on 5% of claims for completeness of the summary. The intake team lead reviews 50 claims a week (stratified by channel, with all scanned-post claims flagged as low confidence), sorting each into acceptable, needs revision or needs override.

    3. Alerts

    AlertThresholdOwnerFirst step
    Correction rateAny field above 3% for a dayAI teamCheck recent prompt or schema changes; pull examples
    SLA riskMore than 50 claims queued over 4 hoursClaims ITCheck model availability and the fallback to handlers
    Decision languageAny occurrenceCompliance and AI teamBlock sending; review the template
    Cost per claim40% above the 30-day baselineAI teamCheck long-document volume and cache hit rate

    4. Feedback. Every "needs override" claim and every corrected field is added weekly to the golden dataset under its slice, and the next prompt or model change must pass them.
