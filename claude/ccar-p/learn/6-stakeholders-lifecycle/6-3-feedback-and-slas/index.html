Feedback Loops, Expectations and SLAs

Exam taskManage stakeholder feedback loops and expectation alignment (including SLAs)

Turn production signals into decisions through a defined feedback loop, decide which signals reach stakeholders, define SLAs as commitments with breach responses, and keep calendar-based governance checks separate from metric alerts.

On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

What you need to know

Monitoring (lesson 4.5) tells you what is happening. A feedback loop decides what happens next, and who needs to know. Without one, dashboards fill up, alerts are ignored, and stakeholders hear about problems from their customers.

The loop

  1. Signals: what is the system showing? Metrics, review findings, user feedback, incidents.
  2. Triage: what needs attention now, what can wait, what is noise?
  3. Decide: does this need an engineering fix, an architect's review, a stakeholder decision, or nothing?
  4. Act: fix, adjust a guardrail, escalate, or change scope.
  5. Review: did it work? Should the trigger or the rule itself change?

Four triage destinations

DestinationWhat goes thereExample
Internal monitoringRoutine engineering signals within agreed limits95th-percentile latency up 50 ms, still under target
Architect reviewSignals that threaten a design assumption or business constraintEvaluation score falling three weeks running; cost per case over budget
Stakeholder reviewAgreed commitments and governance checkpointsSLA breach; scheduled compliance confirmation; scope change
NoiseSelf-resolving or insignificant eventsOne malformed request from a known bad client; a retry that succeeded

Escalating everything to stakeholders is as harmful as escalating nothing: it trains them to ignore you. Agree the routing of each signal in advance.

SLAs are commitments

A service level agreement states:

  • the metric (for example, 95th-percentile response time, measured at the gateway),
  • the threshold and the measurement window (under 3 seconds, per rolling hour),
  • what counts as a breach (two consecutive hours over threshold),
  • the response (who is notified, what is done, by when, and what the customer is told).

Thresholds come from somewhere tangible: user expectations, business criticality, evaluation criteria, agreed budgets. A number the engineering team picked because it was easy to meet is not an SLA. For AI systems, SLAs often include quality as well as speed: an accuracy floor on the evaluation set, or a maximum escalation rate.

Decision rule
Calendar checks run whatever the metrics say

Some governance checks are scheduled obligations: a quarterly output audit, an annual residency confirmation, a periodic bias review. They happen on their date even when every metric is green. Threshold triggers and calendar triggers are different things; a good governance plan has both.

Managing expectations

Stakeholders' expectations drift: early demos set a high bar, a good first month sets another, a competitor's announcement sets a third. Keep a shared, written record of what was agreed (criteria, SLAs, known limitations) and review it at a regular cadence, with production evidence. Surprises should come from the world, not from misunderstandings.

Example
An alert nobody owned

A document assistant's faithfulness score fell slowly for six weeks. Engineering saw it on a dashboard but treated it as "within noise"; the business sponsor never saw it. When a customer complained, the drop had reached 9 points. Afterwards, the team agreed that any three-week decline over 2 points goes to architect review, and any breach of the 94% floor goes to the sponsor within a day.

Exam trap
Waiting for a metric to trigger an audit

If a scenario mentions a scheduled compliance review, the correct answer is to hold it on schedule, even when performance is excellent.

Exam trap
Escalating every alert to stakeholders

Options that send routine engineering signals to business stakeholders create fatigue. Route by the agreed triage categories.

In the exam

These questions describe production signals and ask where each should go, how to define an SLA, or what to do about a scheduled check. Use the loop and the four triage destinations, define SLAs with metric, threshold, breach and response, base thresholds on business needs, and keep calendar-based governance checks separate from metric alerts.

Question 1

All quality and latency metrics have been green for a quarter. A quarterly audit of outputs against the documentation standard is due. What should happen?

Show answer

Answer: B. Calendar-based checks are obligations, not reactions to metrics. Good metrics do not substitute for a required audit.

Question 2

Which item belongs in an SLA definition but is missing from "the assistant must be fast"?

Show answer

Answer: A. An SLA is a commitment: what is measured, the limit, what counts as a breach and what happens then. "Fast" defines none of these.

Question 3 · select 2

Which TWO signals should go to architect review rather than internal monitoring?

Show answer

Answer: A, B. Signals that threaten agreed constraints or design assumptions need architect review. One-off noise and changes within limits stay with engineering monitoring.

Apply it: Write Hollis & Grant's governance table and SLA

Exercise · about 20 minCase: Hollis & Grant Home

Hollis & Grant's assistant is launching. Write the production-signal governance table and the chat SLA the support director will sign.

  1. List at least six signals with their trigger and triage destination (internal monitoring, architect review or stakeholder review).
    Done when: at least one signal is calendar-based.
  2. Write the chat SLA with metric, threshold and window, breach rule and breach response.
  3. Say where each threshold came from.
  4. Describe the review cadence with the support director.

2. Chat SLA. Metric: 95th-percentile time from customer message to first word of the reply, measured at the chat gateway. Threshold: 3 seconds, per rolling hour, during service hours. Breach: two consecutive hours over threshold. Response: on-call engineer engaged within 15 minutes; support director notified within an hour; if not restored within four hours, chat routes to human agents with a status banner.

3. Sources. 3 seconds comes from the case's stated target; the resolution and cost thresholds from the success criteria in lesson 4.1 and the value case in lesson 1.6; the quarterly review from the support director's quality process.

4. Cadence. A monthly review with the support director: SLA performance, quality and cost trends, open issues, and any changes to scope or thresholds, recorded in writing; plus the quarterly sampled review.
