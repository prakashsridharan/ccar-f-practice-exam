Communicating Decisions and Trade-offs

Exam taskCommunicate architectural decisions and trade-offs

Present architectural decisions so stakeholders can act on them, stating what they gain, what they give up and what reversing the decision would cost, in the language of the person deciding.

On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

What you need to know

An architect can understand a trade-off perfectly and still fail to get a decision, because the people deciding do not think in tokens, endpoints or chunking strategies. They need to know what they are choosing between, in terms of the things they are accountable for.

Three elements of every trade-off

  1. What you gain with this option.
  2. What you give up: cost, capability, speed, risk.
  3. What reversal costs: what it would take to change course after the system is built around this choice.

The third element is the one most often left out, and it is what turns apparent agreement into real agreement. A stakeholder who approves an option without knowing it is hard to undo has not really decided.

Example decisionGainGive upReversal cost
Load whole documents instead of building retrievalSimpler to build and launchHigher cost per call as documents growBuilding retrieval later, under production cost pressure
One delivery route for all workloadsSimpler identity, logging and operationsFlexibility for regional or regulatory needsA late residency requirement can block go-live
Lighter logging to save latencyA faster experienceVisibility into individual decisionsRebuilding audit trails after an incident or audit finding

Speak the decider's language

AudienceFrame the trade-off in terms of
FinanceRun cost, payback, cost per outcome, budget risk
Operations leadersThroughput, SLAs, staff workload, peak capacity
Compliance and legalObligations met or at risk, evidence, liability
ExecutivesBusiness outcome, timing, risk, what is irreversible
EngineeringComplexity, reliability, maintainability

A weak answer to "why can't we use one platform for everything?" lists endpoints and regions. A strong answer says: one platform is cheaper to build and run; it limits our ability to meet different countries' data rules; if such a rule arrives after launch, moving regulated workloads could delay rollout by months. "I recommend choosing the primary platform by our strictest compliance need, and adding a second route only where a workload needs it."

Decision rule
Recommend, but make the choice real

Present the options honestly, including the one you would not pick, with all three elements for each. Then give your recommendation and why. A presentation with only one viable option is not a decision.

Demos and proofs of concept

A demonstration built on the stakeholder's own scenario, with realistic (anonymized) data, shows more than a generic one. State one or two known limitations up front: discovering them halfway through damages trust, while naming them shows control of the scope.

Example
Cheaper, until it is not

A team proposed dropping prompt caching work to ship a week earlier. Presented as "gain: one week; give up: about $4,000 a month in run cost; reversal: one week of work later, at any time", the sponsor chose to ship early, knowingly. The same team presented "lighter logging for 300 ms faster responses" with its reversal cost, rebuilding the audit trail for a regulated workflow, and the sponsor rejected it.

Exam trap
Benefits and drawbacks without reversal cost

When a question asks what is missing from a trade-off presentation, the answer is often the cost of changing course later.

Exam trap
Technical framing for a business decider

Options that explain the trade-off in implementation detail to a CFO or business sponsor are weaker than ones that state the business consequence.

In the exam

Trade-off questions describe a decision, an audience and some options. Choose the presentation that states gain, cost and reversal cost in the audience's terms, includes a recommendation, and respects stated requirements (an explicit audit-trail requirement cannot be traded away for speed). Watch for options that omit reversal cost or hide a requirement violation inside a performance gain.

Check your understanding

Question 1

A proposal to cut logging detail for faster responses lists the speed gain and the loss of detail. What else must it state?

Show answer

Answer: D. The reversal cost is the element stakeholders most need and most often do not get. Restoring audit detail after the system depends on the speed gain can be expensive.

Question 2

A CFO asks why the design needs two delivery routes. Which answer is best?

Show answer

Answer: C. It states the business trade-off: lower cost against a regulatory need that one route cannot meet, which is what the CFO needs to decide.

Question 3 · select 2

Which TWO practices make a trade-off presentation decision-ready?

Show answer

Answer: A, C. Decision-ready means real options, all three elements, in the audience's language. Hiding options or limitations produces false alignment.

Apply it: Present Prairie State's SLA trade-off

Exercise · about 15 minCase: Prairie State Mutual

Prairie State's VP of Claims Operations is frustrated: the acknowledgement SLA is still at 90%, short of 98%, because low-confidence extractions wait in the handler queue. The VP proposes letting low-confidence claims go straight through, "since handlers can fix things later".

  1. Lay out at least two options with gain, give up and reversal cost for each.
    Done when: the VP's own proposal is one of the options, presented fairly.
  2. Write your recommendation in two or three sentences addressed to the VP.
  3. Name the evidence you would bring to support it.
Check your work
Show model answer

1. Options

OptionGainGive upReversal cost
A. Low-confidence claims go straight throughSLA likely above 98% at onceWrong fields enter ClaimsCore; more rework later; compliance exposure if wrong policy facts reach customersFinding and correcting bad records after the fact; regulator questions if acknowledgements were wrong
B. Keep the review, but acknowledge first: send a holding acknowledgement within the SLA for every claim, and review fields afterwardsSLA met; data quality protectedA second customer message for some claims; handler workload unchangedLow: a template and a routing change
C. Keep the review and add handler capacity at peak timesSLA improves; quality protectedStaff cost at peaksLow

2. Recommendation. I recommend option B: every customer gets an acknowledgement within a day, which is what the SLA measures, while uncertain claims are still checked before they become records. Option A meets the SLA by moving errors into ClaimsCore, where they cost more to fix and can reach customers. If B does not get us to 98%, we add peak capacity (C).

3. Evidence. The share of low-confidence claims that handlers actually corrected last month and the average rework time per error (35 minutes); SLA performance by day of week; and a sample holding acknowledgement approved by compliance.
