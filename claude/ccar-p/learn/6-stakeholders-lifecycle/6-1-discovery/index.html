Structured Discovery and Requirements

Exam taskConduct structured discovery and requirement gathering

Run discovery as structured elicitation, turn what stakeholders say into requirements, constraints and assumptions, and write it down so every design decision traces back to a source.

On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

What you need to know

Stakeholders describe what they want in their own language: "seamless", "accurate", "safe with our data". Each phrase hides a requirement the architecture must meet, and different readings lead to different designs. Discovery is the work of finding out which.

Listen, translate, write down

  1. Listen for the goal in the stakeholder's words, and for the expectations they did not say aloud.
  2. Translate each statement into a requirement, a constraint, or an open question.
  3. Write it down: requirements with their source, assumptions marked as assumptions, decisions with their reason.

Skipping the third step is how an unstated assumption survives design, build and testing, and surfaces in production or an audit, when changing course is most expensive.

Four questions that structure a discovery session

QuestionEstablishesExample answers
What must it do?Functional scopeDraft the summary; check the policy; route the request
What must it not do?BoundariesNever send without approval; never touch payment data
What must it cost?Budget and capacityRun cost per month; acceptable cost per case; staff time
What must it prove?Evidence and auditWho approved each output; that data stayed in region

The translation table

Stakeholder saysImplied constraintArchitectural consequence
"It has to feel instant"A latency target, and graceful behaviour when slowAgree a 95th-percentile target; stream responses; define the fallback
"Just read the form and send it to the right team"Routing is a business ruleClaude extracts; a rules engine routes
"Someone will check it anyway"A human approval step existsMake the approval gate mandatory and designed, not assumed
"We are a bank, so be careful"Regulatory and audit obligationsIdentify the specific obligations; plan controls and evidence

Requirement or assumption?

A requirement traces to something a stakeholder stated or an obligation that was confirmed. An assumption is something the architect believes but nobody has confirmed: a retention period, a volume, that a system has an API, that users will accept a delay. Assumptions are fine; unlabelled assumptions are not. Record each one with who can confirm it and by when, and treat confirming it as work.

Decision rule
Every requirement has a source

If you cannot point to the person or document a requirement came from, it is an assumption. Label it, and go and confirm it.

Discovery outputs

A good discovery produces a short set of artifacts the design depends on: the translation table, the requirements list with sources, the assumption register, the success criteria (lesson 4.1), and the constraints that rule options out (regulation, approved vendors, budgets, latency).

Example
"Make it accurate"

A legal team asked for "an accurate contract assistant". Discovery found three different meanings: the deal team wanted every obligation and deadline captured (completeness), the general counsel wanted no statement without a clause reference (faithfulness), and IT wanted results in the matter system within an hour of upload (latency). Each became a measurable criterion with an owner, and the "within an hour" item turned out to be an assumption IT had made, not a business need.

Exam trap
Designing from the first sentence

Options that propose an architecture straight from a vague request ("seamless", "smart") skip discovery. The better option asks the questions that turn the phrase into constraints.

Exam trap
Assumptions written as requirements

A design document that states a retention period, a volume or a latency target with no source is presenting an assumption as fact. The right step is to confirm it.

In the exam

Discovery questions present stakeholder statements and ask what the requirement is, which item is an assumption, or what to ask next. Use the four questions, translate phrases into measurable constraints, separate what Claude does from rules that belong to systems, and flag anything without a source as an assumption to confirm.

Check your understanding

Question 1

A design document lists "chat transcripts kept for 90 days", but no stakeholder or policy mentioned retention. How should it be treated?

Show answer

Answer: B. Without a source, it is an assumption. Retention can be a legal obligation, so it must be confirmed before it shapes the design.

Question 2

An operations manager says, "It just needs to read the form and send it to the right team." What is the best translation?

Show answer

Answer: A. "The right team" is a business rule. Claude handles the reading; deterministic rules handle the routing, which keeps it consistent and auditable.

Question 3 · select 2

Which TWO questions belong in a structured discovery session?

Show answer

Answer: B, C. Discovery establishes scope, boundaries, cost and evidence. Model and language choices come later, from those requirements.

Apply it: Run Lakeview's discovery

Exercise · about 20 minCase: Lakeview Regional Medical Center

In a first discovery meeting about discharge summaries, Lakeview's stakeholders said:

  • CMIO: "Doctors should spend much less time on this, and the summaries have to be right."
  • CNO: "Patients shouldn't wait around for paperwork."
  • Privacy officer: "Obviously, this is PHI."
  • A physician representative: "We'll review whatever it writes, but don't make reviewing harder than writing."
  • Health IT director: "It has to work with our EHR, and we can't support another fragile integration."
  1. Build the translation table for these five statements.
  2. Answer the four discovery questions from the statements and the case facts.
  3. List at least four assumptions you would need to confirm, with who confirms each.
    Done when: nothing in your requirements lacks a source.

1. Translation table

StatementImplied constraintConsequence
"Much less time... have to be right"A time target and an accuracy barMeasure minutes per signed summary; faithfulness and medication accuracy criteria (lesson 4.2)
"Patients shouldn't wait"Summaries ready by a discharge deadlineDrafts ready before the morning rounds; target fewer than 1 in 14 discharges delayed
"Obviously, this is PHI"HIPAA obligations, BAA, US residency, auditBAA-covered route; control register (lesson 5.4)
"We'll review... don't make it harder"Mandatory physician sign-off, and a review experience faster than writingReview screen with sources linked; review time measured
"Work with our EHR... not fragile"Integration through supported FHIR APIs, with low support loadUse EHR APIs; fail safe to the manual process

2. Four questions. Must do: draft summaries from EHR notes with medications and pending results from systems. Must not: send unsigned summaries; suggest medication changes; let PHI leave covered services or the US. Must cost: a run budget not yet stated, plus physician review time below today's 35 minutes. Must prove: who signed each summary and when, what sources were used, and residency.
