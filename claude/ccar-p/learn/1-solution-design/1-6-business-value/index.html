    Aligning Solutions to Business Value

    Exam taskAlign solutions to business value pillars (efficiency, transformation, productivity, cost, performance SLAs)

    Tie a design to the value the business actually cares about, express it in the business's own units, and build the cost and SLA case on measured numbers rather than averages and hopes.

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    A technically sound design still fails if nobody can show it is worth paying for. The exam expects you to connect architecture choices to named business outcomes and to defend them with numbers the business recognizes.

    The value pillars

    Most cases rest on one or two of these:

    PillarThe business questionTypical measure
    EfficiencyDo we do the same work with less effort?Handling time per case, cases per analyst per day
    ProductivityCan our people do more, or more valuable, work?Output per person, time freed for higher-value tasks
    TransformationCan we do something we could not do before?New service, new channel, coverage that was not possible manually
    CostDoes it cost less to run than the alternative?Cost per transaction, total run cost against today's cost
    Performance and SLAsDoes it meet the service level we promise?95th-percentile response time, availability, accuracy threshold

    Name the pillar the stakeholder cares about first. A contact-centre director may care most about handling time; a compliance head may care about consistency and audit evidence; a product owner may want a new capability.

    Build the value case in four steps

    1. State today's baseline in a business unit. "Analysts spend 40 minutes per claim", measured from real operational data.
    2. Predict the future state in the same unit. "15 minutes per claim, including review of the draft."
    3. Subtract the run cost, taken from the sizing model rather than from a demo.
    4. State the payback period and how sensitive it is to volume, adoption and accuracy.
    Exam trap
    Three ways value cases go wrong
    • An estimated baseline. If the "before" number was guessed, the claimed gain collapses the moment finance asks where it came from.
    • Assuming full automation when the design keeps a person in the loop. If reviewers check every output, labour is reduced, not eliminated, and the case must say so.
    • Costing from the average request. When a few very large requests drive most of the spend, an average understates run cost and overstates the return.

    Size cost and latency properly

    The sizing model takes call volume (from the business owner), tokens per request as a distribution, the model tier, and sensitivity scenarios. Then look at the levers:

    • Prompt caching cuts cost and latency when a long, stable prefix (system prompt, reference documents) repeats across many requests. Cache writes cost more than normal input, and cached content expires after a short time without use, so infrequent traffic gains little.
    • Batch processing lowers the price substantially for work that can wait, such as overnight document runs. It does not fit interactive, real-time use. For regulated data, confirm the batch route is covered by the same compliance arrangements.
    • A smaller model tier for simpler steps, with an evaluation proving quality holds.
    • Output length limits where long answers add cost without adding value.
    Decision rule
    SLAs are about the tail

    Promise and measure the 95th or 99th percentile, under realistic concurrent load, not the median from a single test request. A design that is fast on average can still breach its SLA every afternoon.

    Example
    Choosing the lever that does the work

    An HR policy assistant answers 80,000 employee questions a month. Every request carries the same 7,000-token block of policy text and instructions, and the projected bill is well over budget. Switching to the largest model makes it worse; raising the output limit does nothing for cost. Caching that repeated block removes most of the repeated input cost and also reduces latency, which is why it is the first lever to pull here.

    In the exam

    Value questions usually give you a stakeholder, a goal and some numbers. The best answer states the benefit in the stakeholder's unit, builds on a measured baseline, includes the real run cost and the human review the design requires, and chooses the cost or latency lever that matches the workload's shape. Be wary of options that promise savings from full automation, quote average latency for an SLA, or pick the most capable model without a reason tied to the stated pillar.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    A value case assumes analysts will stop spending time on claim summaries, but the design keeps a reviewer on every summary. What is wrong with the case?

    Show answer

    Answer: A. When the design keeps a person in the loop, the value case must count the review time that remains. Modeling full automation promises savings that will not appear.

    Question 2

    Which SLA statement fits a customer-facing assistant best?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. SLAs are about the slow tail under real load. Medians, monthly averages and single-request tests all hide the moments when users wait longest.

    Question 3

    A nightly job reconciles 200,000 documents and nobody waits for the results. Which lever cuts cost most directly?

    Show answer

    Answer: C. Work that can wait is what batch processing is for, and it is priced well below interactive calls. Streaming changes perceived speed, not cost, and a larger model raises the price per token.

    Apply it: Build the support value case

    Exercise · about 25 minCase: Hollis & Grant Home

    Hollis & Grant's Finance partner asks for a value case for the routing design from lesson 1.3, scoped to order-status and product questions. Build it in the four steps from this lesson, using the assumed prices on the cases page.

    1. Baseline. Agent hours and cost spent today on the in-scope contact types.
    2. Future state. Assume Claude fully resolves 70% of in-scope contacts (to be confirmed in a pilot) and that each escalated contact saves 2 minutes because the agent receives a summary.
      Why: modelling escalations keeps the case honest about the work that remains.
    3. Run cost. Estimate the monthly model cost at Tier M, including peak season, and check it against the $18,000 budget. Remember that conversation tokens accumulate across turns.
      Done when: you know whether caching is optional or required.
    4. Payback and sensitivity. Payback on the $250,000 build, and the result if resolution is only 40%.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Baseline. In scope: 38% + 21% = 59% of 95,000 = 56,050 contacts a month. Blended handling time 0.55 × 7.5 + 0.45 × 11 = 9.1 minutes. That is about 8,480 agent hours, or $271,300 a month at $32 an hour.

    2. Future state. Resolved: 70% × 56,050 = 39,235 contacts × 9.1 min = 5,930 hours saved. Escalated: 16,815 × 2 min = 560 hours. Total 6,490 hours, about $207,800 a month of agent capacity.

    3. Run cost (per in-scope contact, four turns):

    ItemTokensCost
    Stable prefix, cached, read 4 times24,000$0.007
    Conversation input (2,500 + 5,000 + 7,500 + 10,000)25,000$0.075
    Output (4 × 300)1,200$0.018
    Per contactabout $0.10

    56,050 contacts × $0.10 = about $5,600 a month; at peak (2.5 times) about $14,000. The classifier on all 95,000 contacts at Tier S adds under $100. Within budget. Without caching the prefix, each contact costs about $0.17, and peak reaches about $24,000: caching is required, not optional.

    4. Payback and sensitivity. Net monthly benefit: $207,800 minus $5,600 run cost minus $6,000 platform cost = about $196,000, so the $250,000 build pays back in under 2 months. At 40% resolution, capacity saved falls to about 4,510 hours ($144,400), still paying back in about 2 months.

    The caveat Finance will raise: this is agent capacity, not cash. It becomes savings only if Hollis & Grant changes staffing, avoids peak-season temporary hires, or redeploys agents to work it values. The case should say which.
