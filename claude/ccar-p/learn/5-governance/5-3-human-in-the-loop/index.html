    Human-in-the-Loop Validation

    Exam taskApply human-in-the-loop validation strategies

    Route decisions to people by stakes rather than by volume, place the review where it protects against the real harm, and design the reviewer's job so oversight stays meaningful instead of becoming a rubber stamp.

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    "Put a human in the loop" is not a design. The questions are which decisions need a person, where in the flow the person sits, and what they need to do the job well.

    Route by stakes, not by volume

    Three properties of a decision set the need for review:

    • Reversibility: can a wrong decision be undone cheaply? A draft email can; a sent payment or a denied benefit often cannot.
    • Cost of a wrong decision: a mislabelled ticket costs little; a wrong clinical or financial decision costs a lot.
    • Confidence: how likely the system is to be wrong on this case. Confidence is only useful if it is calibrated against real outcomes; a model can be confidently wrong.
    Decision rule
    The routing rule

    Send a decision to a person when confidence is low and the decision is irreversible or costly. High-stakes decisions may need review even at high confidence; low-stakes, reversible ones usually do not need it at all.

    ConfidenceReversible?Cost of errorReview
    HighYesLowNone; monitor
    LowYesLowNone or sampled; monitor
    HighNoHighOften still required, or sampled at a high rate
    LowNoHighAlways, before the action

    Where the person sits

    PlacementHow it worksFitsCost
    Pre-action approvalNothing happens until a person approvesIrreversible or high-cost actionsLatency and reviewer capacity
    Post-action auditThe action happens; a person reviews afterwards and can reverse itReversible, lower-cost actions at volumeErrors take effect before they are caught
    Sampled reviewA share of decisions is reviewed to measure qualityMonitoring routine workIndividual errors can slip through

    Many systems combine them: pre-approval for the risky band, post-action audit for the middle, sampling for the rest.

    Keep oversight meaningful

    Sending everything to people looks safe and fails in practice. Reviewers facing long queues of routine approvals start approving without reading (consent fatigue or approval fatigue), so the risky cases get the least attention. Design the reviewer's job:

    • Route only what needs judgment, so each review matters.
    • Show the evidence: the inputs, the sources the system used, why it was flagged, and what the system recommends.
    • Make disagreeing easy, and record overrides with a reason.
    • Track reviewer agreement and time per case; a fall in review time with rising volume is a warning sign.
    • Feed overrides back into evaluation (lesson 4.5).

    Diligence is the human counterpart: the reviewer remains accountable for what they approve, which only works if the system gives them what they need to judge.

    Example
    Expense approvals

    A company's expense assistant checks receipts against policy. Claims under $100 that match policy are approved automatically and 5% are sampled. Claims under $1,000 that match policy are paid, then audited weekly. Anything over $1,000, or with a policy mismatch, waits for a manager, who sees the receipt, the matched policy clause and the reason for the flag. Managers now review 6% of claims instead of all of them, and spend longer on each.

    Exam trap
    "Send everything to a human to be safe"

    This fails at scale through consent fatigue and delay. The better option routes by stakes and gives reviewers evidence.

    Exam trap
    Confidence as the only trigger

    A design that skips review for any high-confidence decision ignores stakes. An irreversible, high-cost action can deserve review even when the model is confident.

    In the exam

    Review questions give a decision type, volume, reversibility and cost, and ask which cases go to a person and when. Apply the routing rule, pick pre-action approval for irreversible or high-cost actions, post-action audit or sampling for reversible ones, and prefer designs that give reviewers evidence and avoid flooding them.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    A system drafts replies to internal IT tickets; agents can edit any draft before sending. Volume is 5,000 a day. What review design fits?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Drafts are reversible and low-cost, and agents already see them. Sampling monitors quality without creating a bottleneck; no monitoring at all would hide drift.

    Question 2

    Which decision most clearly needs pre-action human approval?

    Show answer

    Answer: C. Account closure on a weak signal is hard to reverse and costly if wrong. That combination is exactly what pre-action approval is for.

    Question 3 · select 2

    Reviewers approve 99.8% of a growing queue in about four seconds each. Which TWO changes restore meaningful oversight?

    Show answer

    Answer: A, C. The pattern is consent fatigue. Fewer, better-chosen cases with the evidence attached make each review real; more staff on the same queue or reminders do not change it.

    Apply it: Design review for Prairie State's glass fast-track

    Exercise · about 15 minCase: Prairie State Mutual

    Prairie State wants to fast-track payment of small auto glass claims (see the case). Regulation forbids automated denials, not automated payments, but paying a fraudulent or uncovered claim costs money and is hard to recover.

    1. Classify the decisions in the fast-track (pay, refer, deny) by reversibility and cost.
    2. Write the routing rule: which glass claims pay without a person, which go to an adjuster first, and which are audited afterwards.
      Done when: no denial is made by the system.
    3. Define what the adjuster sees when a claim is referred.
    4. Say how you will detect consent fatigue among the adjusters.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Decisions

    DecisionReversible?Cost if wrong
    Pay a covered claimHard to recover once paidAbout $420 each; larger if fraud is organized
    Refer to an adjusterFullyA delay of a day or two
    DenyNot allowed for the systemRegulatory breach

    2. Routing rule

    • Pay automatically when Policy Admin confirms glass cover on the date of loss, the amount is within the policy's glass limit and under $1,000, the fraud engine score is below the threshold, the photo check passes, and the customer has had fewer than two glass claims in 12 months.
    • Refer before paying when any condition fails, when the system's confidence on the photo or invoice is low, or when the amount is $1,000 or more.
    • Never deny: anything that might be uncovered goes to an adjuster.
    • Audit afterwards: 5% of automatic payments are sampled weekly, plus all payments to repairers new to Prairie State.

    3. Referral view. The claim, photos, invoice, extracted fields next to the source, the Policy Admin result, the fraud score with its top factors, the customer's claim history, the specific condition that caused the referral, and buttons to pay, request information, or pass to the special investigations team.

    4. Consent fatigue. Track referrals per adjuster per day, median time per referral, and the share approved unchanged. A falling review time with a rising approval rate triggers a review of the routing rule (too many low-value referrals) before it becomes rubber-stamping.
