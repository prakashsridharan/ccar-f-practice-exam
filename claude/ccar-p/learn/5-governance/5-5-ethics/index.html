    Fairness, Bias and Transparency

    Exam taskAddress ethical AI considerations (bias, fairness, transparency)

    Find the points where an AI system can treat people unequally, measure outcomes by group where lawful, and log decisions so each one can be explained to the person affected, an auditor and an engineer.

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    Fairness is not something an organization can hand to its model provider. Even a carefully trained model sits inside a system of data, prompts, examples and routing rules, and unequal treatment can enter at any of them.

    Where unequal outcomes enter

    Injection pointHow bias gets inExample
    Data and retrievalThe sources over- or under-represent groups, or encode past unequal decisionsA hiring assistant retrieves past "successful employee" profiles from a period of skewed hiring
    Prompt framingWording carries assumptionsA prompt describing "typical reliable applicants" in ways that correlate with background
    ExamplesFew-shot examples come from one group or reflect historical biasEvery example of an approved application has the same profile
    Inputs and processingSome groups' inputs are handled worseHandwritten or non-native-language submissions extracted less accurately, then treated as incomplete
    Routing and thresholdsDifferent groups are sent down different pathsOne group's cases escalated for extra scrutiny more often for the same facts

    Measuring only the final answer misses most of these. Fairness has to be checked wherever the system retrieves, interprets, classifies or routes.

    Measure it

    • Outcome rates by group, where collecting or inferring group data is lawful and done with suitable protections: approval rates, escalation rates, time to decision, error rates.
    • Counterfactual tests: run the same case with only a group-related detail changed (a name, an age, a neighbourhood) and check that the outcome does not change when it should not.
    • Slice the evaluation set (lesson 4.1) by the groups and input types that matter, and treat a gap as a regression.

    Anthropic's own research on discrimination in model decisions uses this kind of approach: systematic variations of the same scenario to reveal differences in treatment.

    Transparency and decision logging

    If a decision cannot be reconstructed, it cannot be explained or investigated. For each decision, keep:

    • the original inputs,
    • the retrieved context that influenced it,
    • the model's output and the configuration (model, prompt version),
    • the routing decision and any human action,
    • an identifier linking all of these.

    Different audiences need different explanations:

    AudienceNeeds
    The affected personWhy this decision was made about them, in plain language, and how to contest it
    Regulator or auditorEvidence that the required process and controls were followed
    EngineersInputs, context, configuration and path, to reproduce and fix

    Discernment

    Reviewers and operators should judge outputs rather than accept them: is this decision justified by the evidence, is the same rule applied consistently, does anything suggest the outcome turned on an irrelevant characteristic? This habit, called discernment in Anthropic's AI Fluency framework, is what turns logging into accountability.

    Decision rule
    Check fairness at every decision point, not just the answer

    Ask, for each step that retrieves, classifies or routes: could this treat comparable people differently? Then measure it.

    Example
    Slower service for handwritten claims

    An insurer's intake system extracted typed claims well and handwritten ones poorly. Low-confidence handwritten claims went to a manual queue that took three days longer. Older customers, who submitted more handwritten claims, were waiting longer on average. No one had designed unfairness; a processing gap plus a routing rule produced it. Improving OCR, and giving the manual queue the same SLA, closed the gap; the slice now appears in every evaluation.

    Exam trap
    "The model is unbiased, so the system is fair"

    Options that delegate fairness to the model provider miss the injection points the architect controls: data, prompts, examples, processing and routing.

    Exam trap
    An aggregate accuracy figure

    A single accuracy number can hide large differences between groups. Prefer options that measure by subgroup and by input type.

    In the exam

    Fairness and transparency questions describe a decision system and an observed or potential disparity, or a request to explain a decision. Locate the injection point, choose measurement by group or counterfactual testing, and make sure decisions are logged well enough to reconstruct. Distractors rely on the provider's training, a disclaimer, or overall averages.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    A loan pre-screening assistant uses ten few-shot examples of approved applicants, all from the same ZIP code area. What is the main risk?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Examples shape judgment. A set drawn from one group can encode that group as the pattern of success; examples must be representative.

    Question 2

    An applicant asks why their application was refused. Logs contain the model's recommendation but not the documents it was given or the rule that routed it. What is missing?

    Show answer

    Answer: C. Without the inputs, context and routing, the decision cannot be reconstructed, so it cannot be explained or checked for fairness.

    Question 3 · select 2

    Which TWO methods detect unequal treatment?

    Show answer

    Answer: A, D. Group-level outcome comparison and counterfactual tests reveal differences that averages and self-assessment hide.

    Apply it: Check Prairie State's claim prioritization for fairness

    Exercise · about 20 minCase: Prairie State Mutual

    Prairie State wants a queue score so the most urgent claims reach adjusters first (see the case). The proposal: Claude reads each claim and scores urgency from 1 to 5 using the description, the attachments and the customer's claim history; the highest scores are assessed first.

    1. Find the injection points where comparable customers could be treated differently.
      Done when: you have at least four, including one in processing and one in routing.
    2. Redesign the scoring to reduce them.
    3. Define the measurements that would show the score is fair.
    4. Define the decision log that lets Prairie State explain a customer's queue position.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Injection points

    • Inputs and processing: handwritten and scanned claims, and claims written in non-native English, are extracted less accurately, so injuries or uninhabitable homes may be missed.
    • Prompt framing: "urgency" left undefined lets the model reward articulate, emotional descriptions over terse ones.
    • Data: claim history as a factor penalizes customers who have claimed before, unrelated to current urgency.
    • Routing: a low score means waiting longer, which compounds any of the above.
    • Examples: if examples of urgent claims come from one channel (web forms), others look less urgent.

    2. Redesign. Define urgency as explicit criteria (injury, uninhabitable home, vulnerable occupant, vehicle needed for medical travel), extracted as structured yes/no fields with the supporting text quoted. Code computes the score from those fields, not the model. Claim history is excluded. Low-confidence extractions are flagged for a quick human check rather than scored low. Examples cover every channel.

    3. Measurements. Time to first adjuster contact and score distributions by channel (email, web, mail) and by language of submission; counterfactual tests rewriting the same claim tersely, emotionally, handwritten and typed, which should get the same score; and the share of truly urgent claims (judged by adjusters afterwards) that were scored urgent, by channel.

    4. Decision log. Claim ID, inputs and attachments references, extracted urgency fields with quoted evidence, extraction confidence, the score and the rule version that computed it, queue position over time, and any human change with its reason. Customer-facing explanation: which urgency criteria applied and how to tell Prairie State if something urgent was missed.
