    How Much Review Is Enough?

    Exam skillReview Depth and Escalation

    Evaluating the risk and accountability profile of an AI-assisted work product and its intended use, including consequence of error, internal-draft versus external-delivery standards, and what must be independently verified, corrected, or escalated for human review.

    Set review depth by what happens if the output is wrong. Consequence, reversibility, audience and regulatory exposure decide whether a quick read is enough, every fact needs checking, or a qualified person must review or take over.

    Written and maintained by Prakash Sridharan, Claude Certified Architect

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    Accountability does not transfer to the tool. Work produced with Claude is your work once you use or send it, held to the same standard as if you had produced it alone. The AI Fluency framework calls this Diligence. The question is not whether to review, but how deeply.

    Four factors decide the depth

    FactorAskRaises the bar
    Consequence of errorWhat does a mistake cost, and whom?Money, health, legal rights, a person's job
    ReversibilityCan it be caught and undone before harm?Sent, filed, published, paid
    AudienceWho will see it?External, executive, regulator, the public
    Regulatory exposureDoes a rule, contract or law govern it?Filings, disclosures, regulated advice

    A practical scale

    OutputDepth
    Internal agenda, brainstorm, personal notesA quick read for sense
    Internal analysis feeding a team decisionCheck key facts and figures
    Client deliverable, board figures, public postVerify every claim; recompute numbers; approval before release
    Medical, legal, financial advice or decisions about peopleReview by a qualified, accountable professional for every item
    Example
    Same data, two outputs

    From one CRM export, a weekly call list for reps needs a light check, while the board revenue slide needs every figure independently recomputed.

    Decide in advance; do not lower the bar quietly

    The do-not-send-without-review list (client deliverables, financially material figures, regulated or confidential content, public and legal statements) should be fixed before the deadline arrives. A good pilot does not lower the standard for high-consequence content, and a dollar threshold does not remove regulatory exposure.

    Iteration versus escalation

    When rounds of prompting stop improving a high-stakes draft, the next step is a person, not another prompt. When the reviewer lacks the expertise (a new regulator, a clinical question), escalate to someone who has it.

    Exam trap
    Passing the checking to the reader

    "The committee will catch errors" or "the client can check the figure" shifts responsibility to people who are not accountable for your work.

    In the exam

    Items describe an output and its use, and ask how much review it needs, who must review it, or what is wrong with a proposal to reduce review. Weigh the four factors. Distractors apply one standard to everything, rely on a good pilot, use a value threshold for regulated work, or trust Claude's own confidence.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    Which output most clearly must not go out without human review?

    Show answer

    Answer: A. A consequential decision sent to a person externally requires review and accountability.

    Question 2

    A team proposes reviewing only customs declarations over $10,000. What is the main flaw?

    Show answer

    Answer: B. Regulatory exposure applies whatever the value.

    Question 3

    Five rounds on a client proposal changed little, and the author is unsure the pricing is persuasive. Next step?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Flat improvement on a high-stakes item signals human judgment.

    Apply it: Set Fairhaven's review rules

    Exercise · about 15 minCase: Fairhaven Unified School District

    The HR team wants one rule for reviewing Claude-drafted answers to staff questions. About 70% are routine (pay dates, holiday schedule); others concern medical leave, disability accommodations and disputes about pay.

    1. Sort the question types by the four factors.
    2. Write the review rule for each group.
    3. Name who reviews and when.
      Done when: routine answers are fast and sensitive ones are protected.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Groups.

    • Routine (pay dates, holidays, forms): low consequence, easily corrected, internal.
    • Benefits figures (deductibles, leave balances): moderate consequence; a wrong number affects choices.
    • Medical leave, accommodations, pay disputes: high consequence, legal duties, sensitive.

    2 and 3. Rules.

    • Routine: an HR generalist reads the draft before sending; answers cite the handbook section.
    • Benefits figures: the generalist checks each figure against the current plan document before sending.
    • Sensitive: the draft goes to the HR specialist, who checks against law and policy and sends it personally; Claude may help with wording only.
