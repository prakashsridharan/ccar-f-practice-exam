    Catching Hallucinations Before They Ship

    Exam skillHallucination Detection and Verification

    Analyzing outputs for unsupported content, including fabricated citations, confident invented specifics, inconsistencies, and bias, and applying verification techniques such as source checking, citation review, and independent confirmation before the output is used.

    Fluent output can contain invented citations, confident specifics, contradictions, bias and claims of actions Claude never took. Learn the signatures, prevent them in the prompt, and verify against an independent source before anything is used.

    Written and maintained by Prakash Sridharan, Claude Certified Architect

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    Claude writes with the same assurance whether it is right or wrong. A fabricated statistic reads exactly like a verified one. You catch hallucinations by knowing their signatures and checking against sources, not by waiting for something to look wrong.

    Signatures to recognize

    PatternWhat it looks like
    Confident invented specifics"31.4% market share" with no source, from a chat without search
    Fabricated citationsAn author, journal and volume that no database can find
    Unsupported claims in a summaryA figure that does not appear in the uploaded report
    Internal contradictions400 customers on page 2, 650 on page 9
    Outdated knowledgeDetailed "obligations" of a law passed after the training cutoff
    Bias from framing"Which features do customers like, especially the new dashboard?" returns praise for the dashboard
    Claimed actions"I've emailed the team" or "I saved it to your Drive" with no connected tool
    Decision rule
    Precision is a warning sign, not evidence

    The more specific an unsourced claim, the more authoritative it looks. Specific numbers, names, quotes and dates are exactly what must be traced to a source.

    Prevent it in the prompt

    • Restrict answers to the provided sources and permit "I don't know" or "not covered."
    • Ask for the exact supporting quote or section for each claim.
    • For current facts, use web search or Research, which return citations you can open.

    Verify before use

    1. Trace each key claim to its source: open the cited page or find the passage.
    2. Check currency: a real citation can still be last year's figure.
    3. Recompute derived numbers (margins, totals) with code execution.
    4. Read long outputs whole for consistency.
    5. Confirm claimed actions in the actual system.
    Exam trap
    Asking Claude to confirm itself

    "Are you sure?" and "add a note that this is verified" are not verification. Neither is regenerating and comparing. Independent confirmation means a source outside the output.

    Grounded citations from Research are checkable, which is their value, but they still need checking: a source can be paraphrased wrongly or taken out of context.

    In the exam

    Items present an output with a suspicious specific, a citation that cannot be found, a contradiction or a claimed action. Pick the step that checks against an independent source, or the prompt change that grounds the answer. Distractors trust confidence, round the number, add a label, average two figures or ask Claude to vouch for itself.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    A citation Claude gave cannot be found in any database. What is the right response?

    Show answer

    Answer: C. Citations from training memory can be invented; an untraceable citation must not be used.

    Question 2

    A 12-page analysis gives two different customer counts on different pages. What follows?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Contradictions show an unsupported claim; neither figure can be assumed correct.

    Question 3

    Claude says it sent an email, but no email tool is connected. What happened?

    Show answer

    Answer: B. Claimed external actions without a tool are capability hallucinations.

    Apply it: Verify Brightwater's press release

    Exercise · about 20 minCase: Brightwater Communications

    A Claude draft press release for a client's new store says: the store is "the largest organic grocery in Southern California"; it "created 120 local jobs"; a quote from the client's CEO; and "shoppers save an average of 18% versus competitors." The release goes to media tomorrow.

    1. List each claim and its risk pattern.
    2. Say how to verify each and who confirms it.
    3. Decide what happens to claims that cannot be confirmed.
      Done when: nothing unsourced remains in the release.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1 and 2. Claims.

    • "Largest organic grocery in Southern California": a superlative with no source. Verify against a published industry ranking or remove; the client must be able to substantiate it.
    • "120 local jobs": a specific figure. Confirm with the client's HR or press office.
    • CEO quote: attributed to a real person. The CEO approves the exact wording in writing.
    • "Average 18% savings versus competitors": a comparative claim about rivals, with legal risk. Requires the client's documented price study; otherwise remove.

    3. Unconfirmed claims are removed or rewritten as statements the client can support ("one of the region's largest organic selections" only if substantiated). Priya Nair approves the final release, and the client approves the quote.
