    Deciding What to Delegate

    Exam skillDelegation and Workflow Redesign

    Evaluating an existing business workflow using Delegation criteria, including task reversibility, stakes, need for human judgment or empathy, and accountability, to assign each step as AI-executed, human-retained, or human-reviewed, and recognizing over-delegation.

    Map a workflow step by step and assign each step as AI-executed, human-reviewed or human-retained, using reversibility, stakes, the need for human judgment or empathy, and accountability. Watch for over-delegation, halo delegation and review gates that quietly collapse.

    Written and maintained by Prakash Sridharan, Claude Certified Architect

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    The AI Fluency framework calls the choice of what to give AI Delegation. Workflow value comes from choosing which steps Claude does, not from handing it everything.

    Four criteria for each step

    CriterionQuestion
    ReversibilityIf the output is wrong, can it be caught and undone before harm?
    StakesWhat does an error cost, and who bears it?
    Human judgment or empathyDoes the step need relationship, care or contextual judgment?
    AccountabilityWho answers for the outcome, and can they exercise that over an AI result?

    Three assignments

    AssignmentFitsExample
    AI-executedReversible, low-stakes, mechanical, easy to checkPulling order details into a summary; checking applications for missing documents
    Human-reviewedClaude adds value, but a person checks before useDrafting customer replies; summarizing messages that may contain urgent signals
    Human-retainedConsequential, irreversible or accountable decisions; empathy-heavy workApproving payments; deciding admissions, claims or grades; handling appeals
    Example
    A refund workflow mapped

    Assembling order history: AI-executed. Drafting the reply: human-reviewed. Approving a large refund or denying one after a dispute: human-retained. Setting next quarter's refund policy: human-retained.

    Recognizing over-delegation

    • Over-delegation: giving AI a step whose risk calls for human ownership, such as unreviewed rejections of applicants.
    • Halo delegation: delegating a riskier step because an earlier one went well (password resets work, so let Claude approve finance access).
    • Collapsed gate: "AI drafts, human reviews" becomes "AI drafts" when reviewers approve without reading. A review nobody really does is no review.
    • Mapping the tools, not the work: assigning steps to whichever Skill exists instead of mapping the workflow first.
    Exam trap
    Low dollar value means low stakes

    A denied $800 claim can matter a great deal to the person, and still carries regulatory duties. Weigh stakes for the affected person, not only the amount.

    Delegating well also means not under-delegating: keeping mechanical extraction by hand wastes people's time.

    In the exam

    Items show a workflow or a proposal and ask which steps fit which assignment, or what is wrong with a redesign. Apply the four criteria to each step on its own. Distractors justify delegation by speed, by past success on a different step, by a dollar threshold, or by drafting quality.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    Account managers now approve Claude-drafted posts without reading them. What has happened?

    Show answer

    Answer: A. An unstaffed review gate is no gate.

    Question 2

    Which question best tests whether a step can be AI-executed without review?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Reversibility is a core delegation criterion.

    Question 3

    Password resets work well with Claude, so a manager proposes letting it approve admin access to finance systems. The flaw?

    Show answer

    Answer: B. Each step is judged on its own criteria.

    Apply it: Map Copperline's complaint workflow

    Exercise · about 20 minCase: Copperline Credit Union

    Member complaints about fees follow these steps: log and categorize the complaint; pull the member's fee history; check the fee against the fee schedule; draft a reply; decide whether to refund the fee; send the reply; record the outcome. A proposal suggests Claude refunds fees under $25 when a complaint "seems valid."

    1. Assign each step and give the deciding criterion.
    2. Explain what is wrong with the proposal.
    3. Define the review gate for the reply (who, what, when).
      Done when: no consequential step is AI-executed without a gate.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Map.

    • Log and categorize: AI-executed (reversible, low stakes).
    • Pull fee history: AI-executed, within the Enterprise workspace and without account numbers in Claude.
    • Check against the fee schedule: human-reviewed (Claude compares, agent confirms).
    • Draft the reply: human-reviewed (external, about money).
    • Decide on a refund: human-retained (financial decision, accountability).
    • Send: human-retained (irreversible once sent).
    • Record the outcome: AI-executed with agent confirmation.

    2. The proposal lets Claude make a financial decision based on its own judgment of validity. That is over-delegation: refunds carry accountability and regulatory weight whatever the amount.

    3. Gate. Who: the member services agent handling the case. What: the fee facts against the account record, the refund decision and the tone. When: before the reply is sent.
