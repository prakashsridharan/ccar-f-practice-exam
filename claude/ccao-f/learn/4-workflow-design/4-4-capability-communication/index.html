    Explaining Claude to Stakeholders

    Exam skillCapability Communication

    Applying capability communication for stakeholders with limited AI familiarity, including what Claude can and cannot reliably do for a proposed use case, the human oversight retained, and realistic expected outcomes, without overstating or understating capability.

    Describe a Claude workflow to people with little AI familiarity accurately. Say what Claude does, what it cannot reliably do, which human checks remain and what outcomes are realistic, without overstating or understating, and calibrated to the audience.

    Written and maintained by Prakash Sridharan, Claude Certified Architect

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    Integrating Claude into a team means explaining it to people who did not build it: managers, clients, a risk committee, frontline staff. Credibility comes from accurate claims. Overstating loses trust at the first visible error; understating stops useful, safe work.

    A bounded description has three parts

    1. What Claude does in this use case, specifically.
    2. The human oversight that remains: who checks what, and when.
    3. Realistic outcomes, with the evidence behind them.
    Example
    Overstated versus accurate

    "Our AI reviews contracts automatically." versus "Claude drafts the first-pass summary and flags clauses that differ from our standard terms; our lawyer reviews and approves every change before signing. Review time dropped by about half in a three-week pilot."

    Phrases that quietly overstate

    • "Fully automated" or "handles X," which removes the human from the sentence.
    • "As good as our best people at everything."
    • "Error-free," "100% accurate," or "no review needed."
    • Predicting exact future numbers. Claude can model scenarios and ranges from stated assumptions; it cannot forecast exact revenue.

    Understating is also inaccurate: "It can't be trusted with anything important" ignores a proven, reviewed use that saves time.

    Calibrate to the audience

    AudienceWants
    Executives and boardsOutcome, oversight in place, risk posture
    Technical or skeptical expertsFailure modes, error rates found, the checks used
    Risk and complianceControls: which outputs are reviewed, by whom, how errors are caught
    Affected staffWhat changes in their work and what they still own
    CustomersWhat the assistant does and when a person takes over

    Report results with their scope

    A pilot result is real but bounded: "50% faster on one team over three weeks, with quality checked on 30 outputs" lets decision makers judge how far to extend it. Dropping the scope overstates certainty.

    Exam trap
    Shifting accountability in the message

    "Anthropic is responsible for any errors" or "the AI decides" misstates who is accountable. The organization owns its outputs.

    In the exam

    Items ask which statement best describes a workflow to a stakeholder, or what is wrong with a claim. Pick the bounded version that names Claude's role, the human check and evidence-based outcomes, pitched to that audience. Distractors overpromise, understate, hide failures from experts, or drop the pilot's scope.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    Which description of a support workflow is most accurate?

    Show answer

    Answer: B. It states Claude's role and names the human check.

    Question 2

    A CFO asks whether Claude can forecast next quarter's exact revenue. Best answer?

    Show answer

    Answer: C. Exact prediction is an expectation mismatch; scenario modeling is real help.

    Question 3

    A skeptical engineering director is reviewing a pilot. What should the briefing include?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Technical stakeholders trust evidence of limits.

    Apply it: Brief Fairhaven's school board

    Exercise · about 15 minCase: Fairhaven Unified School District

    After a six-week pilot at four schools, Claude drafts the monthly parent newsletter from the events calendar in English and Spanish; principals review and approve every issue. Drafting time fell from about 3 hours to 1 hour per issue; principals corrected 2 to 4 items per issue, mostly dates and names. The board has little AI familiarity, and one member asks if teachers will be replaced.

    1. Write a three-sentence description of the workflow for the board.
    2. State the outcome with its scope.
    3. Answer the board member's question accurately.
      Done when: nothing is overstated or understated.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Description. "Claude drafts each school's monthly parent newsletter in English and Spanish from the district events calendar. The principal reviews every issue, corrects dates and names, and approves it before it is sent. Claude does not send anything or decide what families are told."

    2. Outcome. "In a six-week pilot at four schools, drafting time fell from about three hours to one hour per issue. Principals still corrected two to four items per issue, which is why their review stays in place."

    3. Answer. "No. This changes how newsletters are drafted, not who teaches. Principals keep responsibility for every message, and staff time saved goes back to families and students."
