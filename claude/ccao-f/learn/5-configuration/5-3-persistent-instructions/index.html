    Writing Instructions That Hold

    Exam skillPersistent-Instruction Design

    Applying persistent-instruction design to repeated interactions that require consistent behavior, including Project instructions and other persistent instructions, the division of labor between persistent instructions and per-request prompts, and anticipating the use cases and constraints that keep outputs uniform.

    Write persistent instructions precise enough that two people would read them the same way. Put what applies every time in the instructions and what changes in each request, and anticipate the edge cases and limits that would otherwise make outputs vary.

    Written and maintained by Prakash Sridharan, Claude Certified Architect

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    Persistent instructions, such as a Project's instructions, apply to every conversation without anyone remembering to add them. That makes them powerful, and it makes vague ones costly: they fail silently, in every chat.

    Precision test

    Decision rule
    Would two people read it the same way?

    "Be concise" returns two lines for one person and two pages for another. "At most 150 words unless asked for more" is read the same by everyone.

    VaguePrecise
    "Write good summaries.""List each decision as a bullet; list each action as owner, task, due date; mark unassigned actions 'owner TBD'."
    "Follow our standards."Name them: format, length, citation rule, audience, plus one example of a good output.
    "Help patients with their questions.""Scheduling questions only. Refer any medical question to the care team."

    Division of labor

    Persistent instructionsPer-request prompt
    Tone, sign-off, format defaultsToday's document or data
    Guardrails ("never promise refunds"; "flag what the documents do not cover")The specific customer, case or question
    How known edge cases are handledOne-off variations ("this time, casual")
    When an add-on applies (when to include a security appendix)Temporary facts (today's delay at one warehouse)

    Anything typed at the start of every chat is a candidate for persistent instructions. Anything that changes each time belongs in the request.

    Anticipate use cases and constraints

    Outputs vary most on cases the instructions did not foresee. Name the known edge cases and how to handle them, set a fallback for what the source does not cover ("refer to finance"), and say how to adapt for each known audience. A multi-step procedure that the instructions trigger belongs in a Skill.

    Keep changing facts out of rules

    "Sign as Robert Chen, Executive Director" or "use the Q3 template" goes stale silently. Write the rule generally ("sign as the current Executive Director"; "use the current template in knowledge") and keep the changing fact in knowledge.

    Exam trap
    Exceptions by deletion

    When one task needs a different tone, do not delete the default. Name the exception in the instructions or state it in that request.

    In the exam

    Items show instructions and inconsistent outputs, or ask what belongs in instructions versus the request. Choose the measurable, scoped version, the named edge case and fallback, or the right division of labor. Distractors add adjectives, repeat the rule, leave it to Claude's judgment, or remove guidance entirely.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    Which instruction most reliably changes meeting summaries?

    Show answer

    Answer: C. Precise, testable instructions change output.

    Question 2

    What belongs in the per-request prompt rather than persistent instructions?

    Show answer

    Answer: A. Instructions hold what applies every time; the request holds what changes.

    Question 3

    "Be concise" gives answers from two lines to two pages. Best fix?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. A measurable rule is read the same way by everyone.

    Apply it: Rewrite Copperline's member-reply instructions

    Exercise · about 20 minCase: Copperline Credit Union

    Member services' Project instructions read: "You are a helpful assistant for Copperline. Be professional and friendly. Answer member questions well." Replies vary in length, sometimes promise fee refunds, and occasionally quote account details back to members.

    1. Name the gaps in the current instructions.
    2. Rewrite the instructions.
    3. List what stays in each request.
      Done when: two agents would get consistent drafts for the same question.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    1. Gaps. No format or length; no guardrails on refunds or account data; no edge cases or fallback; vague tone.

    2. Rewritten instructions.

    • Purpose: draft replies to members' written questions for an agent to review before sending.
    • Format: greeting with the member's first name, answer in at most 120 words, one clear next step, sign-off "Copperline Member Services."
    • Tone: warm and plain; acknowledge frustration in one sentence when the member is upset.
    • Guardrails: never promise or imply a refund, fee waiver or credit decision; write "an agent will review this" instead. Never include account, card or Social Security numbers.
    • Edge cases: fraud or a locked account: tell the member to call the fraud line now. Complaints: acknowledge and say an agent will respond within one business day.
    • Fallback: if the fee schedule in knowledge does not answer the question, say so and flag it for the agent.

    3. In each request. The member's message, first name and the agent's notes on the case.
