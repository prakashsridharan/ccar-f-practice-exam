    Choosing a Model and Effort Level

    Exam skillModel and Effort Selection

    Evaluating Claude model tiers and effort levels against a task's quality, speed, and cost requirements, including matching tier to task complexity and stakes, recognizing when the faster, lower-cost option is the right choice, and defending the selection against the alternatives.

    Match the model tier and effort level to the task's complexity, stakes, speed and cost. Use faster, lighter settings for structured, high-volume, low-stakes work, more capability and effort for ambiguous, consequential work, and be able to defend the choice.

    Written and maintained by Prakash Sridharan, Claude Certified Architect

    On this pageWhat you need to knowIn the examCheck your understandingApply itSources

    What you need to know

    Claude models come in tiers, from fast and efficient to most capable. Effort is a separate setting: how much thinking Claude applies to a response. Higher capability and higher effort tend to give more thorough answers on complex work, but take longer and use more of the plan's allowance. Model names change; the decision logic does not.

    Matching tier to task

    Task profileTypical choice
    Classification, extraction, formatting, short templated replies, high volume, easy to correctFast tier (such as Haiku), default or low effort
    Most drafting, synthesis and analysisBalanced tier (such as Sonnet)
    Ambiguous inputs, many interacting factors, high-stakes deliverablesMost capable tier (such as Opus), higher effort

    Recognize when faster is right

    A clear checklist applied to 800 standard agreements, with a lawyer reviewing every flag, is structured work: a fast tier is defensible because the human gate, not the tier, protects the outcome. "It's legal work" or "it's about people" does not by itself require the top tier; the complexity and stakes of the specific step do.

    Defend the choice

    A defensible choice ties each task to evidence:

    1. Test the candidate tier on a few real examples against the quality standard.
    2. Keep the lower setting if it meets the bar; move up only if it does not.
    3. Explain the trade-off: quality needed, speed, and usage or cost.
    Example
    One team, two settings

    Ticket tagging runs on the fast tier: high volume, low stakes, easy to fix. Quarterly strategy memos run on the most capable tier with higher effort: complex and consequential. Using one setting for both would overspend on tagging or underdeliver on strategy.

    Managing usage

    Usage limits apply across time windows, and higher tiers and effort consume more. For long, intensive work, reserve high settings for the hardest steps, use lighter ones for routine steps, and break the work into stages.

    Exam trap
    Model change as a cure-all

    A bigger model does not fix missing context, stale knowledge or wrong arithmetic. Change the tier when the task's complexity, not the specification, is the limit: a clear, complete prompt that still returns a shallow answer.

    In the exam

    Items describe a task, its volume, stakes and the current setting, and ask for the best tier or effort, or the strongest argument for a choice. Look for structure and volume (lean faster) versus ambiguity and consequence (lean more capable), and whether a human gate exists. Distractors use the top tier "to be safe," use one setting for everything, or remove review because a tier is accurate.

    Check your understanding

    Question 1

    600 short emails must be sorted into five clear categories; a misfile is easy to fix. Which choice fits?

    Show answer

    Answer: A. Structured, high-volume, low-stakes work suits the fast tier.

    Question 2

    A clear, complete prompt about a complex pricing model returns a shallow answer on the fast tier at low effort. What next?

    Show answer

    Answer: C. When the specification is complete, the setting limits depth.

    Question 3

    What does raising the effort level generally do?

    Show answer

    Answer: D. Effort trades speed and usage for thoroughness and is separate from the tier.

    Apply it: Set Copperline's model choices

    Exercise · about 10 minCase: Copperline Credit Union

    Copperline uses Claude for: (A) tagging 6,000 member messages a month by topic, reviewed by agents; (B) drafting replies to routine questions about branch hours and card activation; (C) a quarterly board briefing comparing three new regulations and their interactions with existing rules, reviewed by compliance.

    1. Choose a tier and effort for each.
    2. Write one sentence defending each choice.
    3. Describe how you would confirm the choice for task A.
      Done when: each choice cites complexity, stakes and volume.
    Check your work
    Show model answer

    A. Fast tier, default effort. High volume, clear categories, agents review, misfiles easy to correct.

    B. Fast or balanced tier, default effort. Short, templated, low-stakes replies; agents review before sending.

    C. Most capable tier, higher effort. Ambiguous, interacting regulatory arguments with board-level stakes justify depth; compliance still reviews.

    Confirming A. Run 200 labeled messages through the fast tier, compare with agents' tags, and keep the tier if agreement meets the team's standard; move up only for categories where it falls short.
